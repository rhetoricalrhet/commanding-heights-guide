PrologueRavi↗ in the original

Abundant intelligence comes to Bangalore

The essay does not open with a thesis. It opens with one man's day.

5:56 a.m. Ravi wakes before his wife in a fifteenth-floor apartment in Bellandur. He is a graduate of IIT Madras, the son of a scooter mechanic from Hubballi who told him to work with his mind, not his hands. He stayed in India when classmates left for the West, and rose through a firm that does back-office accounting for American insurers.

His work has ballooned. He now oversees delivery for five North American clients instead of one or two, but he no longer manages people. He checks the output of AI agents, built on Chinese models, and lately approves their reports without reading them. Graduate hiring has been paused for a year. Three of his team of fifteen have left, and none will be replaced. Margins are rising, and he is earning more than ever.

9:30 a.m. The local stock index opens at a record. On a call, the chief executive of the firm's American parent announces a restructuring: Ravi's division is cut in half, effective immediately, and staff across the subsidiary will fall by ninety percent over the coming months.

10:21 p.m. He gets home late. Riots have spread across Indian cities as layoffs mount, and youth unemployment in Bangalore has climbed from five percent to thirty. His daughter is studying with an AI tutor that costs almost nothing and is teaching her faster than her school did, toward what, he no longer knows. From the balcony, behind a new concrete wall and razor wire, Ravi and his wife watch smoke rise over the city and, for the first time in months, reach for each other's hands.

Ravi returns in Part V, and his daughter closes the essay.

Retold in summary from the essay's opening. The authors' own telling is longer and in their words.

iThe argument in brief↗ in the original

Intelligence is the giveaway. The conduit is the business.

Every great technology has paid its inventors less than it paid the people who controlled its distribution. Gutenberg went insolvent. The rail fortunes went to men who never built a locomotive. Manidis and Roberts argue that machine intelligence will follow the same path, faster. Frontier models are copied within months, so the price of any given capability collapses toward the cost of the computing power that runs it.

The money, they argue, will settle in a few positions that intelligence has to pass through to reach the wider economy. Those positions are scarce, controllable and durable. Borrowing Lenin's phrase from 1922, the authors call them the Commanding Heights. The essay sets out six connected propositions.

It is not only an investment thesis. It is also a warning. The same forces that create the Heights break the ladder of development for countries like India, close the door on emigration, and widen the gap between owners and everyone else. In the world the authors describe, there are only “sovereigns, exarchates, and the wretched.” The essay keeps both in view, and so should anyone reading it.

vThe scissorsOverture · Part II↗ in the original

Costs rise, prices collapse

The lab's dilemma in a single chart.

Each new frontier model costs several times more to train than the last. Meanwhile, competition and distillation push the price of last year's capability toward the cost of compute. The authors ask who could expect trillions in spending to be repaid in twenty-dollar subscriptions. Their answer is that durable margin will not come from the model layer.

viThe LinePart II↗ in the original

Four curves that are really one

Capex, the index, training cost and capability. The authors say they have always been the same line.

In the last quarter of 2025, investment in information-processing equipment added more than three times the growth of the entire US economy. Retirement accounts ride on the index, and the index rides on a few AI companies. That makes a stall politically intolerable. The authors expect Washington to sustain the Line with state equity in the labs and, eventually, by capping Treasury yields, as the Fed did from 1942 to 1951. As AI debt spreads, its correlation with the rest of the economy approaches one. Two bonds with the same rating may then fail together.

Why the authors think so Washington capping its own borrowing costs

The precedent is the Second World War. In 1942 the Federal Reserve fixed Treasury bill yields at ⅜% and capped long-term yields at 2½%, buying whatever investors wouldn't. The arrangement lasted until the Treasury–Fed Accord of 1951.

The essay's reasoning: if the frontier becomes a matter of national defense, and the state ends up financing training costs that grow about 3.5× a year, its borrowing needs will push yields up. Once those yields start constraining the program, the authors expect Washington to suppress them again, even if Congress has to change the Fed's mandate to do it.

viiTwo sovereignsPart III↗ in the original

Intelligence splits into three tiers

The best models stop being for sale, and nationalization happens without being called nationalization.

Sovereign frontier

Air-gapped and run like a defense program, in a lab that answers only to the state.
Not for sale

Commercial frontier

Premium American models, always priced against Chinese ones.
US leads
~10.6× premium

Commodity intelligence

Open-weight models priced near the cost of compute. They set the floor for everyone.
China sets
the floor

Why would China give its models away? Look at where each country keeps its savings. Chinese households hold their wealth in housing and bank deposits, so the party-state has little reason to defend high valuations for its labs. American retirements depend on the stock market, which now depends on AI labs. Cheap open models from China therefore hit the American financing model directly, and keeping the frontier funded may fall to the American state.

Why the authors think so Nationalization without the word

The labs need things only a state can supply: permission to release their strongest models, predictable rules on foreign access, help with power and permits, financial certainty, and political legitimacy for gains that flow to few holders. A sovereign shareholder removes risks no private investor can.

  • Tools short of seizure: a single “golden share” (as in the 2025 U.S. Steel deal), procurement contracts, cost-plus programs, and licenses for frontier training runs.
  • Precedents: Lockheed building Polaris for one customer; the Atomic Energy Commission taking over the Manhattan Project's labs in 1947.
  • China: already there. Model developers register with the Cyberspace Administration and stay under continuous review.

The likely result, in the essay's view, is a lab split in two under one roof: a commercial company that sells at whatever margin the Chinese floor allows, and a frontier lab with a direct line to the White House.

viiiThe Neo-DollarPart IV · Superscription↗ in the original

America's strongest AI asset is its currency

“Whose is this image and superscription?” The coin, the authors suggest, still answers to Washington.

The essay follows one payment. Malena, a freelance designer in Buenos Aires, triples her client list with an AI model. Clients pay her in a dollar stablecoin she keeps because the peso loses a third of its value each year. The issuer holds Treasuries, so she is helping fund US debt without ever having had a US bank account.

56.7%The dollar's share of world FX reserves, Q2 2026
89.2%Share of currency trades with the dollar on one side
~70%Share of global AI compute owned by five US hyperscalers
~5%Share owned by Chinese firms, end of 2025

The second purpose of the Neo-Dollar is quieter. Beijing taxes savers by holding deposit rates down. Washington taxes them through inflation, and because the dollar is the world's money, part of that bill is paid abroad. A channel the economy must pass through earns revenue that rises with prices without needing matching investment. The authors see that as one more reason to own one.

Why the authors think so Exporting inflation

A reserve currency lets its issuer debase and share the cost with everyone abroad who holds it. Billions of people already save in dollars out of preference and habit, and stablecoins extend that to people no American bank would serve.

The stablecoin itself pays its holder no interest. The income on its Treasury reserves goes to the issuer, at rates Washington sets. The essay sets this beside older, more visible takings: FDR's 1933 gold order, and the paydown of war debt at rates below inflation after 1945. The new version is quieter, and it reaches Buenos Aires as easily as Boston.

Ravi, two months later

Two months after the riots began, Ravi's keycard stops working. A machine-drafted letter tells him his job has moved to the “agentic services tier.” He gets six months' severance, and the parent company's stock opens higher in New York. The letter does not mention that his months of approving agent output were the training data for his replacement. His government offers him reskilling, perhaps in mechanical or farm work.

Summarized from Part V.

Why the authors think so The emigration door closes

For poor countries, emigration was the release valve for an educated surplus. In January 2026 the EU and India signed a mobility pact promising up to 100,000 multi-year work permits. The essay doubts it survives: Britain, France and Germany all hold general elections within three years, with anti-migration parties strong in each.

The deeper reason is economic. As AI reduces demand for surplus labor, rich-country citizenship becomes more valuable and voters become less willing to share it. The authors note that an American passport already sells for $5 million and ask how generous anyone will be when it costs $50 million.

xHeight or bottleneck?Part VI↗ in the original

A Height is not a bottleneck

In 1922 Lenin let private trade back into Russia because the state still held “all the Commanding Heights.”

A bottleneck is a temporary shortage. A data center without a grid connection hands its margin to the power company until power is solved. Then the shortage moves to transmission, then to chips, and the margin moves with it. The authors call this endless cycle a “financialized Samsara.” A Height is different: it keeps a lasting share of the activity it enables, and that activity makes it stronger.

Bottleneck

  • Scarce for now
  • High margins draw new supply
  • The margin moves to the next shortage
  • Priced for the cycle

Commanding Height

  • Hard to route around
  • Its take grows with the economy above it
  • Its revenue rises with inflation without matching new investment
  • It survives regulators, local politics and the sovereign

xiThe fourth settlementPart VII · The Firm↗ in the original

Management dissolves. Operators become capital.

Each era answers four questions: who supplies the capital, who decides, who does the work and who is owed.

The essay follows Guinness from a leased Dublin brewery in 1759 to a brand inside Diageo. Ronald Coase explained that firms exist because markets are costly to use. Intelligence lowers those costs and makes work legible, so labor leaves the firm. Offshoring was the rehearsal. What remains is a small group of operators, too valuable to pay as employees, who demand to be paid and governed like capital.

xiiFor the allocatorParts VII–VIII · Alpha and Omega↗ in the original

Buy DoubleClick and Android, not Alphabet or Anthropic

Investors make two mistakes: they hold the last era's Heights and finance this era's inventions.

The essay's investment conclusion is blunt. The safe compounders in most portfolios are this era's newspapers. The fashionable AI basket is stuck in Genesis, mistaking itself for a Height. Meanwhile the ten-year Treasury yield, at 5.17%, has broken above its long-run average of about 4.5% since 1871. The authors read that as the end of a forty-year bond bull market, which means no more help from falling rates. Returns will have to come from owning the right positions.

The rail eraPennsylvania Railroad

The world's most valuable company in 1912. Its workforce peaked in 1920, and in 1970 it filed what was then the largest bankruptcy in US history.

Print and searchNewspapers → Google Ads

Hearst tolled the spread of news, and Google took over that toll at far greater scale. Now the queries are leaving for AI agents.

Closing↗ in the original

Out from the garden, never to return

The essay ends where it began, with a K that opens at every level. Ravi's daughter can reach the machine whenever she likes. She might reject it, or live the same life faster, or become something new. Perhaps the many in the middle will live long, content lives. The few who see and act, the authors write, will become a new shape of their own, and the rest of us their subjects.

Then the frame widens. Technology has a direction, from cuneiform to the assembly line to the first atomic test in the New Mexico desert, and now to machine intelligence. They ask whether its makers felt what every parent knows: that the child may surpass them. We are heading, they write, toward a city at the edge of sight. They don't say when we will arrive, only that the road passes through older and harder human conditions before something entirely different.

Their last instruction is to accept that the world as you knew it has already ended, and to prepare for what comes after.

AppendixGlossary & open questions

The essay's vocabulary

Genesis
Everything that designs, trains, hosts and serves models. The place AI is born, and in the essay's view not where its value ends up.
The Line
AI capex, stock indexes, training costs and capability, read as a single rising curve the US economy now depends on.
The Artifact
A trained model: billions to make, cheap to copy, and quickly distilled into commodity models.
Distillation
Training a cheaper model on a stronger one's outputs. It is how the frontier leaks into the commodity tier.
Sovereign frontier
The best models, withheld from the market and run for the state like a weapons program.
Peculiar labs
After England's “royal peculiars,” which answered to the Crown alone. Here, labs that answer only to the state.
Sinomodernity
China's form of modern governance. It can publish open models to set a global price floor while holding strategic ones in reserve.
Neo-Dollar
The digital, stablecoin dollar carried by apps to people outside the US banking system, together with the firms that carry it.
Superscription
The inscription on a coin, from the Gospel “render unto Caesar.” Shorthand for whose authority a currency carries.
Exarchate
Any country other than the US or China: nominally independent, but dependent on one superpower's technology.
Crossing
A point activity must pass through. Margin pools there, though most crossings are temporary.
Settlement
One era's arrangement of who supplies the capital, makes the decisions, does the work and is owed.
K-shape
An economy in which one group rises as another falls. The essay expects the split to widen at every level.
The Guild
Licensed professionals kept on payroll because a liable human must sign for the work.
Commanding Heights
Scarce, controllable positions that durably capture the gains of increasingly capable, widely used AI.
Questions to argue aboutfrom this guide, not the authors
  1. If models become commodities, which distribution channels can AI itself not route around?
  2. Does the Neo-Dollar thesis survive strict stablecoin regulation, or a rival digital currency?
  3. How long will Beijing keep publishing near-frontier open models, and what would make it stop?
  4. Is quiet nationalization of the labs more likely than open public ownership?
  5. What line in an earnings report would reveal a Height the market has not yet priced?
